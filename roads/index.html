Topic 01 · Roads

Roads & Pavement Condition (PCI)

Petaluma tracks street condition using the Pavement Condition Index (PCI), a 0–100 score. As of the most recent regional survey, the city's network averages around 50 (MTC's 2024 three-year moving average), placing it in the "At Risk" band and below the Bay Area's weighted average of 67.1 Older city documents still cite a PCI of 42, sourced to a 2019 survey — a five-year-old figure, not the current one.2

What PCI measures

PCI comes from a standardized visual survey (ASTM D6433) of cracking, patching, rutting, and other distress, converted into a single 0–100 score.3 The score maps directly to what kind of repair a street needs:

Why letting a road degrade doesn't save money

The claim: skip maintenance now, save the money (or invest it), and pay the larger repair bill later — for less overall. This doesn't hold up, for three reasons.

1. The cost jump isn't linear — it's a step change

A road stays watertight while it's in good condition, so preventive treatment is cheap resurfacing. Once cracking lets water into the base layer, the base itself fails, and the fix changes from "seal it" to "rebuild it." That's a 26–30x jump in unit cost between prevention and full reconstruction, not a gradual increase.4

2. The deterioration curve accelerates before the money would even come due

Pavement condition doesn't decline steadily — it holds up reasonably well, then drops fast. One regional pavement study places that acceleration point ("the cliff") around PCI 55–57.6 Petaluma's network average (~50) is already past that point — meaning further deferral is happening in the steepest, most expensive part of the curve, not a stable holding pattern.

3. The savings have to actually be saved — and they weren't

For "defer and invest" to beat "maintain on schedule," the deferred money would need to earn a return that outpaces a 26–30x cost multiplier over the deferral period. No realistic investment return does that. More importantly, the underlying assumption — that deferred maintenance money gets saved and invested — doesn't match what happened in Petaluma: the city's own budget document attributes its backlog directly to "funding shortfalls over many years," not deferred savings.2 The debt is now being financed through bonds at roughly 2.85% interest, meaning the city pays financing costs on top of the inflated repair cost — the opposite of a savings outcome.2

The one narrow exception: once a road has already dropped to roughly PCI 20–25, the repair type (full reconstruction) no longer changes with further delay, so the repair cost is effectively fixed. At that point, deferring it isn't a mistake — it's freeing up budget for streets where the money still buys a cheaper fix. This is different from deferring a road still in the 40–60 range, which is the most expensive place to delay, not the cheapest.4
